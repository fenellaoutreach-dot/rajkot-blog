From Temple Flowers to $6 Million Funding: The Unusual Success Story of Phool

Rajkot.blog editorial team · 28 November 2026 · India Business & Innovation

A discarded flower rarely looks like the beginning of a manufacturing business. Yet Kanpur-based Phool has built a company around collecting used floral offerings and turning them into saleable goods. On 16 November 2026, the Earthshot Prize Impact Assembly in Mumbai announced a new US$6 million debt-financing deal for the Indian startup. Its story offers a striking example of how waste collection, product development and mainstream finance can come together.

Important distinction: The $6 million is debt financing, not annual revenue, profit, valuation or a prize award.

How Phool began: a problem on the Ganga

According to ETTextile's 24 November report, co-founder Ankit Agarwal began investigating discarded flower offerings after seeing them enter the river in Kanpur in 2015. Agarwal and Prateek Kumar subsequently founded the venture in 2017, as confirmed by IIT Kanpur's 18 November announcement.

The business challenge was not simply inventing a product. It required convincing temples to participate, establishing collection arrangements, sorting the material and finding buyers for the processed goods. A stream of unwanted flowers is only a useful input if it can be collected and handled reliably.

From incense to a leather alternative

Phool's early products included incense made from recovered floral material. It later developed Fleather, a biomaterial intended as an alternative to animal leather. The Earthshot Prize finalist profile describes the material and its development. The project reached the 2022 Earthshot Prize finalist stage in the waste-reduction category. This recognition was not the same as winning the prize.

For a business reader, the interesting distinction is between selling a finished consumer product such as incense and developing a material that could be supplied to other manufacturers. These are different markets, with different requirements for quality, production capacity and customer approval.

What changed in November 2026?

The Earthshot Prize announcement dated 16 November identified Exim Bank, SIDBI and ICICI Bank as the lenders behind the $6 million deal. It also reported that daily processing had risen from 36 tonnes in 2022 to 50 tonnes, with operations extending across four states.

Five business lessons for entrepreneurs in Rajkot

1. Start with a recurring problem. A material that is discarded repeatedly can be a research opportunity, but its quantity, contamination and seasonal variability need testing.

2. Build the supply chain first. Collection agreements, transport costs and consistent input quality may determine whether a waste-to-product concept can operate economically.

3. Validate one product before expanding. A simple consumer product and an industrial biomaterial may need very different machinery, technical expertise and sales channels.

4. Verify performance. Customers need evidence of durability, safety and consistency. Environmental marketing claims should be supported by appropriate testing and not assumed from the use of recovered material alone.

5. Understand the financing. A loan is a source of capital with repayment obligations. It does not make a business profitable by itself.

Rajkot's engineering and manufacturing businesses could apply these questions when exploring local material-recovery opportunities. Frequently asked questions

What is Fleather?

It is Phool's flower-derived material developed as an alternative to conventional animal leather.

Did Phool win $6 million at Earthshot?

No. The financing was announced at the November 2026 Impact Assembly and came from three banks. Phool was an Earthshot finalist in 2022.

Is this a Rajkot company?

No. This is an Indian business innovation feature about a Kanpur-based enterprise, with lessons for Rajkot readers.
